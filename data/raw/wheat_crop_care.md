## Section 1: Critical Irrigation Stages in Wheat
Wheat requires balanced soil moisture rather than continuous standing water. In educational agronomy references, crown root initiation (around 20 to 25 days after sowing) is considered the most moisture-sensitive stage because primary root establishment determines tiller density. Secondary critical windows include late tillering, booting, flowering (anthesis), and the milk-to-dough grain-filling stages. Excess waterlogging in poorly drained soils reduces root aeration and can stunt early growth.

## Section 2: Recognizing Leaf Yellowing and Nutrient Indicators
Lower-leaf yellowing (chlorosis) starting from leaf tips on older wheat foliage is commonly associated in extension literature with nitrogen limitation or temporary waterlogging stress. In contrast, interveinal yellowing on younger upper leaves may point to micronutrient availability constraints in high-pH soils, such as iron or zinc limitations. Because visual symptoms frequently overlap, educational guidance emphasizes confirming nutrient status through soil or tissue testing with a local agricultural extension laboratory before making amendment decisions.

## Section 3: Scouting for Wheat Rust and Foliar Diseases
Wheat rusts—including yellow (stripe) rust, brown (leaf) rust, and black (stem) rust—are fungal diseases monitored through routine field scouting. Stripe rust typically appears as linear rows of yellow-orange pustules along leaf veins during cool, humid weather, whereas leaf rust forms scattered orange-brown pustules. Preventive cultural concepts include selecting locally adapted resistant varieties, avoiding excessive late-season lushness, and removing volunteer cereal plants between seasons. For localized management thresholds, growers are advised to consult regional extension advisories.

## Section 4: Post-Harvest Moisture and Grain Storage Hygiene
Clean, dry storage protects harvested wheat from storage molds and grain weevils. Educational post-harvest guidelines highlight drying grain to safe storage moisture levels (typically around 12 percent or below for medium-term storage), cleaning bins thoroughly to remove old grain residue, and monitoring stored grain temperature periodically to detect moisture migration early.
