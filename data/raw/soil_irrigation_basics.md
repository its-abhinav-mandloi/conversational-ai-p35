## Section 1: Mulching and Soil Moisture Conservation
Organic mulches—such as clean straw, dried leaves, or composted plant residue—reduce surface evaporation, moderate soil temperature extremes, and cushion soil aggregates against heavy raindrop impact. Keeping a thin mulch layer slightly away from direct contact with tender plant collars helps prevent stem rot in humid climates.

## Section 2: Soil pH and Nutrient Availability Overview
Soil pH governs how readily plant roots can absorb dissolved mineral nutrients. Most annual field and vegetable crops absorb major nutrients efficiently in a slightly acidic to neutral pH range (roughly pH 6.0 to 7.0). Strongly acidic or alkaline soils can lock up phosphorus or micronutrients even when total soil reserves are present. Representative composite soil sampling sent to an accredited testing center provides the baseline needed before applying lime, sulfur, or fertilizers.

## Section 3: Drip vs. Furrow Irrigation Efficiency Principles
Drip (trickle) irrigation delivers water slowly near the root zone, minimizing evaporation loss and keeping foliage dry—an advantage for reducing foliar fungal spread. Furrow irrigation channels water along ridges between plant rows and requires careful land leveling to avoid uneven percolation or tailwater runoff. Regardless of method, checking soil moisture a few inches beneath the surface avoids unnecessary watering.

## Section 4: Integrated Pest Management (IPM) Core Principles
Educational IPM frameworks organize crop protection as a tiered decision process: (1) prevention through healthy soils, resistant cultivars, sanitation, and crop rotation; (2) regular field observation and accurate symptom identification; (3) mechanical, cultural, or biological controls before chemical interventions; and (4) consulting qualified local extension specialists whenever targeted interventions are considered.
