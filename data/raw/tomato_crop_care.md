## Section 1: Early Blight vs. Late Blight Visual Indicators
Tomato foliage diseases are commonly distinguished during scouting by lesion pattern and progression. Early blight typically begins on older lower leaves as dark brown spots with concentric rings ("target-board" pattern) surrounded by a yellow halo. Late blight progresses rapidly under cool, wet conditions as water-soaked, pale green to dark grayish-brown blotches on leaves and stems, sometimes showing white fungal-like growth on leaf undersides in high humidity. Removing severely affected lower leaves for airflow and avoiding overhead evening irrigation are standard preventive cultural concepts.

## Section 2: Blossom-End Rot and Calcium-Moisture Balance
Blossom-end rot appears as a sunken, dark, leathery patch at the bottom (blossom end) of developing tomato fruits. Educational horticulture materials note that this physiological disorder is most often triggered by uneven soil moisture that disrupts calcium transport to rapidly expanding fruit, rather than an absolute lack of calcium in the soil. Consistent mulching and steady irrigation scheduling help stabilize root-zone moisture uptake.

## Section 3: Leaf Yellowing, Root Stress, and Pruning Hygiene
Yellowing of lower tomato leaves can stem from natural senescence, shading in dense canopies, overwatering/poor drainage, or vascular wilt conditions. Pruning lower suckers and sanitizing pruning tools between rows reduces mechanical spread of pathogens. Staking or trellising keeps foliage off damp soil and improves ventilation.

## Section 4: Flowering Temperature Sensitivity and Pollination
Tomato flowers are sensitive to extreme heat or cold nights; sustained daytime temperatures above roughly 35 degrees Celsius or cool nights below 13 degrees Celsius can reduce pollen viability and cause blossom drop. Educational guides recommend wind airflow or gentle vibration in protected structures and adequate soil moisture during peak bloom.
